--- layout: single title: "Teaching & Mentorship" permalink: /teaching/ author_profile: false --- ## Teaching Philosophy My teaching philosophy is grounded in the belief that students learn science most effectively through active inquiry, experiential learning, and collaborative problem-solving. Growing up in New York City, I became aware of disparities in access to educational resources and opportunities for hands-on scientific engagement. These experiences shaped my commitment to creating accessible learning environments that encourage curiosity, critical thinking, and scientific independence. I aim to help students connect biological and geological concepts while developing the confidence to investigate questions about Earth's history, life, and environments. ## Teaching Through Inquiry and Active Learning I encourage students to engage directly with scientific evidence through observation, hypothesis testing, and collaborative activities. At the University of Illinois Urbana–Champaign, I developed laboratory exercises incorporating paleobotanical, herbarium, and entomological collections to teach concepts in evolution, morphology, and biodiversity. One activity challenged students to reconstruct ancient ecosystems by developing hypothetical dinner menus using fossil plants from selected geological intervals. Through these exercises, I encourage students to apply scientific concepts creatively while developing their ability to interpret evidence and communicate their findings. ## Field-Based Education Field-based education is central to my approach to teaching ecology and environmental change. In Illinois, I developed field activities examining plant and insect diversity in tallgrass prairies, allowing students to investigate how habitat structure and environmental conditions influence community composition. As teaching faculty with the Juneau Icefield Research Program, I developed lessons examining primary succession in recently deglaciated environments and island biogeography on nunataks. These experiences integrated field observations, ecological surveys, hypothesis testing, and data interpretation to connect scientific theory with processes occurring in natural environments. ## Mentorship My approach to mentorship emphasizes intellectual independence, individualized guidance, and professional development. I believe students, particularly graduate students, should have the freedom to pursue research questions they are passionate about while receiving the support necessary to develop their ideas. I have mentored twelve undergraduate researchers and one graduate student in paleobiology, statistical modeling, and machine learning. Through meaningful research responsibilities, constructive feedback, and opportunities to communicate their findings, I aim to help students develop the confidence and skills needed to pursue their scientific and professional goals.
