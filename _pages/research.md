<a id="decomposition"></a>
## 01 · Plant–arthropod interactions and decomposition

**How did plant–arthropod interactions shape decomposition in ancient peat-forming ecosystems?**

I use coprolites, borings, and other feeding traces to investigate how terrestrial arthropods fragmented and processed plant material in Pennsylvanian peat forests. Coprolite morphology and associated plant tissues help infer feeding behavior, including among small-bodied arthropods rarely preserved as body fossils.

My research has examined more than six thousand coprolites and developed systematic frameworks for twenty-six morphotypes. These datasets help distinguish herbivory and detritivory and identify stages of organic-matter transformation before burial.

Modern comparisons are essential to this work. I investigate insect feeding damage on mangrove foliage in the Florida Keys and processes of litter accumulation and decomposition in South Florida wetlands, including the Everglades. These comparisons provide ecological context for interpreting ancient peat-forming environments.

<a id="specialization"></a>
## 02 · Trophic specialization and resource partitioning

**How did trophic specialization and resource partitioning shape ancient food webs?**

A second focus examines how herbivory and detritivory influenced niche partitioning and the organization of feeding guilds. I compare coprolite morphology, plant associations, and resource use across Pennsylvanian assemblages to investigate ecological specialization.

This work includes evidence from coprolite-filled borings and reconstructions of feeding-guild changes during the Middle–Late Pennsylvanian Floral Transition. By combining feeding traces with network analyses and comparisons to living arthropods, I investigate how plant-resource availability and floral turnover influenced food web structure.

<a id="change"></a>
## 03 · Environmental change and ecological restructuring

**How does environmental change restructure terrestrial food webs through time?**

My third research theme asks how ecological interactions reorganize during environmental disruption and recovery. Changes in plant communities can alter feeding opportunities and trophic relationships even when taxonomic turnover alone does not reveal the full ecological response.

I compare fossil plant assemblages and feeding traces across stratigraphic and environmental gradients to investigate network structure, specialization, and recovery. My ongoing Florida Keys mangrove work also examines herbivory across environmental gradients, with planned repeat surveys following hurricane disturbance.

Together, fossil and modern datasets provide complementary perspectives on ecological restructuring and resilience.

## Research approaches

**Fossil collections and microscopy** · Coal ball peels, fossil plant tissues, coprolites, and feeding traces

**Digital imaging and quantitative methods** · Computed tomography, high-resolution imaging, morphotype classification, and network analysis

**Modern ecological comparisons** · Mangrove herbivory, wetland litter processing, and field surveys
