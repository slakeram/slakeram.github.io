<div class="research-story">

  <section class="research-story-opening" aria-labelledby="research-intro">
    <h2 id="research-intro">Understanding ancient ecosystems</h2>
    <p>Plants and arthropods have shaped terrestrial ecosystems for hundreds of millions of years. Through feeding and decomposition, these interactions influence how ecosystems function and respond to environmental change. Yet much of their early evolutionary history remains poorly understood.</p>
    <p>My research investigates how plant–arthropod interactions shaped ancient ecosystems, particularly the peat-forming forests of the Pennsylvanian Period. By studying fossil feeding traces and comparing them with living ecosystems, I examine how ancient food webs were organized, how organisms used plant resources, and how these relationships changed through time.</p>
  </section>

  <section aria-labelledby="fossil-evidence">
    <p class="research-story-eyebrow">THE FOSSIL EVIDENCE</p>
    <h2 id="fossil-evidence">Windows into ancient ecosystems</h2>
    <div class="research-story-feature">
      <div class="research-story-copy">
        <h3>Coal balls: Preserving ancient forests</h3>
        <p>Coal balls are mineralized masses of ancient peat that preserve plant tissues in remarkable anatomical detail. Unlike many fossils that preserve external impressions, coal balls can reveal the internal structures of roots, stems, leaves, and other plant organs.</p>
        <p>They provide a rare glimpse into the plants that formed Pennsylvanian peat forests and the organisms that interacted with them.</p>
      </div>

    <div class="research-story-feature research-story-feature-reverse">
      <div class="research-story-copy">
        <h3>Coprolites: Evidence of ancient feeding</h3>
        <p>Coprolites are fossilized fecal material that preserve evidence of what ancient organisms consumed. Their shapes, internal structures, and preserved plant fragments provide clues about feeding behavior that would otherwise be difficult to recognize in the fossil record.</p>
        <p>They are particularly valuable for studying small arthropods whose bodies are rarely preserved. I have examined more than 6,000 coprolites and developed systematic frameworks for 26 morphotypes to investigate herbivory and detritivory.</p>

  <section aria-labelledby="network-explainer">
    <p class="research-story-eyebrow">FROM FOSSILS TO ECOLOGY</p>
    <h2 id="network-explainer">Reconstructing ancient food webs</h2>
    <p>Fossils preserve more than individual organisms. They can also provide evidence of the relationships between them. By identifying plant tissues within different coprolite morphotypes, I investigate which resources were consumed and how they were used.</p>
    <p>Bipartite networks help visualize associations between coprolite types and plant resources. These patterns provide a framework for examining resource use, feeding specialization, and the organization of ancient food webs. Potential arthropod producers are inferred from the fossil evidence rather than always directly identified.</p>

  <section aria-labelledby="research-questions">
    <p class="research-story-eyebrow">QUESTIONS THAT GUIDE MY WORK</p>
    <h2 id="research-questions">Three central research questions</h2>

    <article class="research-story-question" id="decomposition">
      <span class="research-story-number">01</span>
      <div>
        <h3>How did arthropods contribute to decomposition in ancient forests?</h3>
        <p>Decomposition is essential to the recycling of organic material, yet the role of arthropods in this process remains poorly understood in ancient peat-forming environments. I study coprolites, borings, and other feeding traces to investigate how arthropods consumed and processed plant material before burial.</p>
      </div>
    </article>

    <article class="research-story-question" id="specialization">
      <span class="research-story-number">02</span>
      <div>
        <h3>How did arthropods share plant resources in ancient food webs?</h3>
        <p>Not all organisms consume the same resources. I examine plant tissues preserved within different coprolite morphotypes to investigate patterns of resource use and potential feeding specialization. Comparing these associations across fossil assemblages helps reveal how feeding relationships changed as ancient forests evolved.</p>
      </div>
    </article>

    <article class="research-story-question" id="change">
      <span class="research-story-number">03</span>
      <div>
        <h3>How did ancient food webs respond to environmental change?</h3>
        <p>Environmental change can alter not only which organisms inhabit an ecosystem, but also how they interact. I compare fossil plants and feeding traces across environmental transitions, including the Middle–Late Pennsylvanian Floral Transition, to investigate how ecological interactions reorganized during disruption and recovery.</p>
      </div>
    </article>
  </section>

  <section aria-labelledby="modern-ecology">
    <p class="research-story-eyebrow">CONNECTING PAST AND PRESENT</p>
    <h2 id="modern-ecology">Modern ecological comparisons</h2>
    <p>Modern ecosystems offer valuable comparisons for interpreting processes preserved in the fossil record. My research in the Florida Keys examines insect herbivory on mangrove foliage across environmental gradients. I also investigate plant litter, arthropod activity, and organic-matter accumulation in South Florida wetlands.</p>
    <p>These comparisons connect feeding, decomposition, and resource use in living ecosystems with the long-term history of terrestrial ecological interactions.</p>
