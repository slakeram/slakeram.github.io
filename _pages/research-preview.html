RESEARCH PROGRAM

Understanding the evolution of terrestrial ecosystems

Plant–arthropod interactions across deep time.

Plants and arthropods have shaped terrestrial ecosystems for hundreds of millions of years. Through feeding and decomposition, their interactions influence how ecosystems function and respond to environmental change. These interactions play an important role in the organization of food webs, the formation of soils, and the cycling of carbon. Yet the evolutionary history of these ecological relationships remains poorly understood.

The fossil record provides a unique opportunity to investigate these ancient interactions. Coal balls, mineralized deposits that preserve ancient plants at cellular resolution, offer evidence of feeding activity within plant tissues. Fossil leaves also preserve traces of arthropod feeding, including holes, tunnels, and galls.

By examining fossil feeding traces alongside modern ecological systems, my research investigates how plant–arthropod interactions have shaped the evolution and functioning of terrestrial ecosystems.

How do arthropods contribute to decomposition and soil formation?

Arthropods play an important role in breaking down plant material and recycling nutrients. Coprolites (fossilized fecal material) preserve evidence of these activities in ancient forests. Comparisons with plant-litter decomposition in modern wetlands help reveal how arthropod activity influences soil formation, peat accumulation, and carbon cycling.

How have plant–arthropod interactions shaped food webs?

As plants evolved and diversified, new resources became available to arthropods. My research uses fossil feeding traces and ecological networks to examine how different organisms utilize plant resources. Comparisons across geological time provide insight into the evolution of feeding relationships, specialization, and the organization of food webs.

How do ecosystems respond to environmental change?

Changes in climate and vegetation can alter feeding relationships and restructure food webs. Comparisons across ancient environmental transitions reveal how ecological relationships change through time. My ongoing research in the Florida Keys examines insect feeding on mangrove foliage across environmental gradients, providing a modern perspective on how these interactions respond to changing conditions.
